A tenfold jump in an AI time horizon can mean different things

A time horizon sounds like a stopwatch. Here it measures the human time for tasks an AI can solve with 50% probability.

Drew Nguyen and William Fithian reanalyse METR's 228 software tasks and 26 AIs. Their statistical fit lets task difficulty change nonlinearly with human time. The fitted difficulty curve is almost flat between two and 30 minutes.

So a jump from three to 30 minutes and one from 30 minutes to five hours both look like 10x. In this fit, they represent different increases in difficulty.

This matters when reading a capability chart. The authors recommend showing diagnostic plots alongside the headline horizon. They explicitly do not challenge the observed exponential trend over release dates.

The public preprint was submitted on 8 October. These are estimates on this software task collection; the paper does not establish the same curve for other work.

BrickBench asks an agent to design something you can build

Give an agent a LEGO design prompt. It has to pick real parts, connect them and produce a buildable assembly.

BrickBench introduces 300 prompts across small models, larger sets and builds restricted to a fixed inventory. BrickAgent gives coding agents part search, rendering and physical checks.

The checks cover collisions, connections and simulated stability. The benchmark also scores whether the design matches the prompt and how good it looks. Keeping those scores separate helps show where an agent still struggles.

Leading agents satisfy many physical and semantic requirements. Yet human raters identified the human design in 323 of 360 comparisons. That is recognition, not a measured preference rate.

A small bridge illustrates the problem: the pieces need to join, the structure needs support and it still needs to resemble the requested bridge. This is an illustration, not an extra experiment.

Submitted 8 October. The paper links a benchmark release, but its project page did not load in this check. Physical validity here uses the benchmark's validators and simulation.

OnTrack watches an agent while the run is still happening

An agent can spend a long time repeating the same failed move. Reading the log afterwards won't recover that compute.

OnTrack compares each new step and its dependencies with successful reference runs. It can flag loops, stalls and departures from the expected process during execution. The authors report about a millisecond per step for monitoring.

On SWE-agent traces, a candidate stopping policy saved around 18% of compute on failing runs at roughly 83% precision for interrupted runs. That denominator matters: this is not an 18% saving across every run.

For example, repeated searches without progress could trigger a warning before another expensive round. That is a concept example, not a reproduced deployment.

The full paper is unusually clear about the limits. Once trace lengths are matched, its generic trajectory signals do not reliably predict patch correctness. False stops remain possible and other stopping policies can match the savings.

Submitted 8 October; public preprint. Reference quality and threshold calibration matter before deployment.

HANS keeps the crossed out work in the OCR problem

A handwritten maths answer has more than a final number. There are steps, corrections, tables and old work crossed out along the way.

HANS describes 5,213 answer images from 745 students across 18 problem types. Its annotations preserve the solving process and mark correction noise. The authors say personal identifiers were cropped.

Their NA-GOT method suppresses noisy regions in the visual features and in the decoder's attention. The paper reports improved recognition on HANS.

Suppose a student crosses out one equation and writes a corrected version below. A transcription should retain the active step in the right order without treating the deleted line as part of the answer. That is a simple illustration of the parsing task.

The useful focus here is recognition of the whole working process. It does not establish that an automated system can grade students reliably.

Submitted 8 October. The preprint is public; the authors promise dataset availability upon publication. A downloadable release is not verified yet. Results come from this collection rather than broad classroom validation.

MASS trains a model on how its agents work together

The handoff between agents can become training data too.

MASS alternates between searching for better multi-agent workflows and fine-tuning a shared model on the resulting conversations. Copies of the same model act as solver, evaluator and workflow optimizer. The updated model then starts another cycle.

Across two cycles with Qwen3.6-27B, the authors report 1.2 to 1.6 times higher benchmark performance per output token on four open-ended research benchmarks. This ratio does not include the full cost of workflow search and training.

One possible workflow has an agent write code, another inspect the result and an orchestrator bring their findings together. MASS learns from both the local work and the coordination. That is a conceptual example.

The final evaluation uses external model judgments withheld from workflow optimization. Even so, shared model errors remain a risk in the self-supervision loop. Two cycles do not establish unlimited improvement.

Submitted 8 October. The public paper describes the training setup. Its code link returned 404 in this check; results have not been independently reproduced here.
